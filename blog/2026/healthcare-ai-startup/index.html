Why We Quit Our Healthcare AI Startup—Yet Believe Saudi Could Lead the Field

Created in July 26, 2026

2026

We walked away because we could neither fit within today’s healthcare market nor disrupt it—but AI will, and Saudi Arabia has the rare incentives and capabilities to lead what comes next.

Or, a longer summary…

The simplest way I can summarize what we learned is that healthcare is not merely a large market with difficult customers. It is a complex system held together by its structure, incentives, and internal dynamics. Those forces are not background conditions; they decide which problems can become businesses, which products can enter, and what kinds of companies can scale. The more ambitious ideas often imagine a different healthcare system, but building them means changing an equilibrium that exists for rational reasons. That requires more than good technology. It requires leverage over the system itself. The company we wanted to build did not fit the current equilibrium, and we had no credible way to change it, so we walked away.

Still, that is a conclusion about building a company today, not about the future of healthcare. The current system was built around scarce medical intelligence and the difficulty of coordinating it. AI changes both. If intelligence becomes scalable, healthcare will eventually have to reorganize around it. The race will therefore be won not only by whoever builds the best models, but by whoever can turn them into care across an entire system. Saudi Arabia has a rare chance to lead because the need to transform, the ability to move institutions together, and the foundations required to execute are all arriving at the same time. Walking away from the startup while becoming more optimistic about the field was not a contradiction after all.

1. Introduction

Recently, after months of exploration and experimentation, Mohammed and I decided to wind down our healthcare AI startup project and pursue a different direction.

Exploring healthcare as outsider technologists was one of the most humbling experiences of my life. The longer we spent in the field, the more I realized how little of the system was visible from the outside. What initially looked like unnecessary friction often turned out to be a rational response to sensitive data, clinical risk, conflicting incentives, old infrastructure, and the difficulty of coordinating large institutions. My early impatience gradually became empathy for the people working within those constraints.

We learned this because many people were generous enough to help us. Government leaders, hospital executives, doctors, operators, investors, and many others gave us their time, answered our questions, and pointed out what we could not yet see. The list is too long to include without inevitably leaving someone out, but I am deeply thankful to everyone who helped us understand the field.

Those conversations also made me optimistic about the future of healthcare in Saudi Arabia. I met ambitious leaders and talented people who are not merely discussing transformation but doing the difficult work required to make it happen. We eventually concluded that this particular venture was not the right path for us, but that decision did not reduce my belief in the sector or in the people transforming it.

I am writing this essay to document what we learned along the way. These are not conclusions from an exhaustive study. They are observations from two outsiders who spent months trying to understand the market well enough to build within it. I hope they are useful to people already working in healthcare, and especially to other technologists trying to enter it.

The essay has three sections. The first explores how the healthcare market works today and why promising technologies can be difficult to turn into viable companies. The second looks at how AI may change healthcare, why Saudi Arabia has an unusual opportunity to lead that change, and what could help accelerate it. The third tells the story of our exploration: what we tried, what we misunderstood, and why we eventually decided to walk away.

2. Why Learning How Healthcare Really Works Made Us Walk Away

2.1. Why the Obvious Opportunities Start to Break?

Healthcare AI is one of those opportunities that looks almost too obvious from the outside. AI is becoming capable of doing tasks across medicine and healthcare that once depended entirely on people, healthcare AI startups in the US are raising large rounds, and healthcare costs continue to rise. Here in Saudi Arabia, we face many of the same problems, several local ones, and an entire sector already undergoing transformation. It seemed that all we had to do was find the right problem.

A VC partner later told me that our understanding was still entirely first-order and that, once we understood the market, we would pivot again. I thought I understood what he meant. I did not.

One market, three realities

Ambient AI is a useful case study in why this first-order view breaks. It listens to the conversation between a doctor and a patient and prepares the clinical note automatically. Doctors globally complain about documentation, and almost every doctor we interviewed locally complained about it too. Then you look at a company like Abridge and the rest seems proven: AI can solve the problem, and someone can build a large business around it.

If the problem exists here and the technology works elsewhere, it is tempting to treat localization as the remaining task. But that assumes the Saudi healthcare market is one place in which to sell the product. It is not.

The public sector represents most healthcare activity in the country. Many public hospitals operate under heavy demand, so reducing the time required for each encounter could help them serve more patients. The sector is also undergoing a major transformation, and the leaders we met had a genuine appetite for technology. On the level of national need, ambient AI appeared to fit almost perfectly.

Yet need does not determine where a startup can begin. Public contracting is designed to reduce delivery and institutional risk, so it naturally favors established suppliers with guarantees, resources, and previous deployments. An unproven startup usually needs unusually defensible technology to justify that risk, while ambient AI was becoming a crowded category. Public providers could be excellent partners for validating its value, but it was almost impossible to build a pre-seed startup sales strategy around winning their contracts.

The smaller private providers appeared to offer the opposite trade-off. There were many of them, their owners were easier to reach, and decisions could move faster. But smaller budgets limited what they could buy, while fragmented systems made every deployment different. Ten clinics might produce ten contracts, but also ten sales efforts and ten integrations. Access improved precisely where leverage weakened.

Large private providers seemed to resolve the tension. They had meaningful budgets and enough patient volume for efficiency gains to matter. But there were only a handful, and the number kept shrinking as we added practical conditions: the right workflow, accessible infrastructure, and some willingness to trust a new company.

What looked from the outside like one large market was therefore three different markets. Each contained healthcare providers, but each implied a different company: public-sector contracting, long-tail clinic software, or relationship-heavy enterprise sales to a small group of large providers. This is why market size alone says surprisingly little. Market structure determines which opportunities are reachable and what business model can exist around them.

The market was large. The viable starting market was not.

Pain is not an opportunity

Suppose we found the right provider. Almost every doctor we had interviewed wanted relief from documentation. Was that not enough to establish the opportunity?

In one conversation, an executive asked us a simple question: Were the doctors already fully booked? Our financial case assumed that every minute saved would become another appointment and therefore more revenue. But if the schedule had empty slots, saving the doctor a few minutes created no additional visit. The user gained time; the hospital captured no financial return.

The question revealed a mistake in how we had defined the problem. We treated the hospital as if it were one person experiencing the doctor’s pain. In reality, the doctor used the product, the medical office cared about clinical quality and physician satisfaction, IT carried the integration and security risk, and finance or procurement controlled the budget. The same product could create value for all of them, but not necessarily enough value for any one of them to own the purchase.

The incentives could even reverse. A busy public hospital might value the extra capacity, while a doctor on fixed compensation might experience the saved time as more clinical work for the same pay. A private hospital with unused capacity might have enthusiastic doctors but little economic reason to buy. The product had not changed. The opportunity had.

This is also why strategic narratives can be misleading. A provider may genuinely support patient-centric care, digital transformation, or Vision 2030 and still decline a product aligned with those goals. Strategy describes where an organization wants to go. Purchasing follows the problems, owners, and budgets that exist today.

We had been asking whether a product that worked in the US could also work here. The executive’s question exposed a more important test: Did the same incentives exist here? Technology can cross borders almost unchanged; the opportunity around it cannot. Opportunities do not transfer across markets. Incentives do.

That distinction pushed us toward a different kind of AI problem: managing rejected insurance claims, which sits closer to the hospital’s finances and fits its existing incentives more directly. AI can identify the coding or documentation errors that cause a claim to be rejected. In that case, revenue the hospital had already earned becomes a visible loss, a specific team is responsible for recovering it, and finance can measure every improvement. Unlike saved documentation time, the value does not depend on another appointment materializing. The path from the problem to the buyer is simply shorter.

A problem becomes an opportunity only when the value reaches someone who has both a reason and the ability to buy.

The product is the workflow

Even clear value and a willing buyer did not complete the opportunity. Ambient AI can produce an excellent note in a demo, but a hospital does not buy a note floating on a screen. The product needs patient context, approval to touch clinical data, and a place inside the Electronic Health Record. Until the output enters the workflow safely, the AI is only a feature.

When we mapped the large providers, we found that some owned their EHR while others depended on closed local or global systems. Two hospitals could describe the same problem and want the same result, yet one might support a direct integration while the other required months of work through a third party. Technical feasibility was not simply a property of our model. It depended on who controlled the infrastructure around it.

This changed the meaning of product development. In a normal software model, the product is built and integration helps deliver it. Here, integration could consume most of the engineering work and determine whether the product worked at all. Healthcare businesses are built around workflows, not isolated features; in many cases, integration is the product.

The deeper problem was repeatability. An expensive integration did not guarantee a sale, and the work might be useless for the next provider. We could customize the system to win one hospital, then customize it differently for the second, until a supposedly scalable product became a collection of implementations. Solving the same problem several times did not mean we had built the same product several times.

This made the first customer unusually consequential. It would shape the architecture, workflow assumptions, references, and path to expansion. A good first customer could become a wedge into the market. The wrong one could pull the company toward custom services before repeatable software had the chance to exist.

A wedge, then, is more than a narrow first feature. It must solve a problem someone will buy now, fit the surrounding workflow, and be repeatable enough to open a larger market. Otherwise, it is an entry project, not an entry strategy.

So finding something a hospital would buy once was not enough. We needed something we could sell twice without rebuilding the company around the second customer.

Credibility before capability

There was still one assumption left: if we built the product well enough, the right hospital would let us prove the rest. This felt natural to us as technologists. Capability was the thing we knew how to produce.

Healthcare evaluates the problem in the opposite order. Before examining how impressive the AI is, a hospital must ask whether the company can be trusted with patient data, regulated operations, and material that may enter the medical record. A prototype can demonstrate capability. It cannot demonstrate a history of safe adoption.

This produces a difficult loop for a new entrant. Hospitals want references and previous deployments, but the first deployment is how a company earns them. A clinical champion helps, but cannot decide alone: the product must survive IT, management, finance, and procurement, each evaluating a different risk. Access is therefore not a step that follows product development. It is one of the scarce resources from which the product is built.

What initially looked like unnecessary resistance gradually appeared rational. Healthcare leaders had seen endless demos, failed pilots, and vendors who understood the technology but underestimated the system. The cost of moving too slowly is missed improvement; the cost of moving too quickly can involve clinical care, sensitive data, and critical operations. Friction is partly how the system manages that asymmetry.

The result is that credibility comes before capability receives a fair hearing. A technically strong product cannot compensate for missing institutional trust at the starting line. For a new entrant, the scarce asset is therefore not only the technology, but a credible design partner willing to help produce the evidence the rest of the market requires.

Capability could create the product. It could not, by itself, create the conditions for adoption.

By then, ambient AI looked very different from the opportunity we first saw. The doctors’ pain was real. The technology worked. The market was large. None of those observations was wrong; they were simply incomplete.

We had begun by asking one question: Can AI solve a real healthcare problem? The answer was clearly yes. But a startup had to pass two more tests: Could that solution become a repeatable business, and could we enter the market well enough to build it? Ambient AI passed the first test. The market structure, incentives, infrastructure, and burden of trust made the other two much less obvious.

That was what our first-order view had missed. We had focused on the problem and the AI because those were the visible parts. Basically, the opportunity was not the product. It was the entire system required to make the product work as a business.

Once we understood that system, we had to ask a different question: Were we the right team to build within it?

2.2. Which Kind of Healthcare AI Company Can Actually Be Built?

Once we understood that the opportunity was larger than the technology, another question kept coming up: What kind of healthcare AI company could actually be built here?

We did not sit down one afternoon and arrange the market into three neat options. I wish our thinking had been that efficient. But every idea we explored eventually pulled us toward one of three kinds of company.

Building the medical intelligence

One place AI can create value is inside medicine itself: diagnosis, medical reasoning, clinical decision support, or personalized treatment. These systems do not just make a hospital more efficient. If they work, they allow medicine to do something it could not do before. Unsurprisingly, this was also the possibility that excited us most.

Diagnosis was one of the ideas that first pulled me into healthcare. At first, it looked like a difficult but recognizable AI problem. The system would reason across symptoms, medical histories, scans, and test results, then help a physician reach a better conclusion. Hard, certainly, but hard in the direction we liked.

Then we asked a less exciting question: What would it take for a physician to trust the conclusion? Suddenly, the model was only one item on a much longer list. We needed proprietary clinical data, specialized medical research, clinical validation, research partners, substantial funding, and enough time to prove the system on real patients.

I initially thought of these as things surrounding the technology—important, but possible to add later. They were actually part of the technology. A medical model cannot be separated from the data that teaches it, the clinicians who validate it, or the institution willing to carry the risk of testing it. Building frontier medical AI began to look less like adding intelligence to software and more like building a research institution.

The Saudi market adds another tension. Focus on one rare disease or specialty and the research becomes more manageable, but the local market may become too small to support the company. Expand toward general diagnosis and the market returns, but so do global laboratories and technology companies with years of research, data, and clinical partnerships. The more ambitious the opportunity became, the more infrastructure it required before the startup could even begin.

We happened to expose this problem very clearly. We had strong general AI skills, but no medical AI research record, proprietary clinical data, healthcare credibility, or research institution behind us. Unfortunately, models do not train on ambition.

There was an exception to all of this. A genuine research breakthrough could be important enough to overcome a weak starting position. Breakthroughs happen; that is why research is exciting. But depending on one is not much of a market strategy. This kind of company can be built locally, but its natural starting point is a team that already sits at the intersection of research, clinical practice, and patient data—or an ecosystem designed to place it there.

Building the future system

If building new medical intelligence required too much at the starting line, another option was to build around where healthcare was already going. Patient-centric, preventative, personalized, and continuous care sound like separate ideas, but they all describe a similar shift: care organized around a person’s health over time, rather than a series of isolated visits to a hospital.

The direction was easy to believe in. It matched the global movement toward value-based care and the goals of Saudi Arabia’s health transformation. We imagined entering through Front Desk AI, a conversational system for intake, navigation, and follow-up, then expanding into a much larger healthcare platform. Patient access would lead to provider integration, which would lead to more services, which would eventually reshape the patient journey. On a roadmap, everything connected beautifully. Startup roadmaps often do.

The trouble started when we asked who would buy the first piece. Patient access belonged to the provider. Integration crossed several departments. The value was spread across patient experience, less administrative work, better long-term care, and products we might build later. Almost everyone could agree that the direction was good. That did not mean anyone had an urgent problem and a budget for beginning it today.

The pattern repeated as we looked deeper. Continuous monitoring could prevent a future complication, but the provider had to pay today while the saving might appear months later, possibly elsewhere in the system. Better navigation created value across several departments, but no single one captured enough to own the purchase. Preventative care could reduce long-term costs while current budgets still revolved around treating the visit in front of the hospital.

This helped explain why the future had remained the future for so long. The limitation was not a lack of good ideas. Patient-centric care asked providers to spend, coordinate, and change operations today for value that was delayed, distributed, or captured by someone else.

A startup therefore had to change the equation. It could invent a business model that let the buyer capture the long-term value, own enough of the operation to reorganize the workflow, reach patients through a new channel, or find another source of leverage. Any of these could create a real company. But finding one was the insight, not a detail to solve after choosing the vision. Without it, “healthcare is moving in this direction” was a prediction, not a strategy.

Healthcare makes this harder because changing one behavior often means moving several institutions, departments, regulations, and workflows. The startup is no longer entering a market; it is disrupting a complex system. Our lack of distribution and credibility made this even less likely, but it was not the core problem. We had no concrete way to make the future pay for itself in the present.

Building for the current system

The third option began from the opposite question: What does a hospital already have a reason to buy? That points away from redesigning healthcare and toward operational software—workflow automation, cost reduction, revenue protection, and regulatory work. In our case, it led to Revenue Cycle Management (RCM), the work through which a provider turns the care it delivers into collected revenue.

Consider a rejected insurance claim. A hospital provides care and submits a claim, but an error in coding, documentation, or process causes the insurer to reject it. Revenue the hospital expected to collect becomes a visible loss. A specific team owns the problem, finance can measure it, and AI can identify the errors and patterns causing it.

Compare that with the earlier ideas. Ambient AI had to turn saved documentation time into another appointment before the hospital captured financial value. Front Desk AI spread its value across several departments and future outcomes. With rejected claims, the loss had already happened. The path from the problem to the buyer was short enough to see without an elaborate diagram, which was refreshing.

The difference became visible in the conversations. With earlier ideas, we spent much of the meeting explaining why the problem should matter. With RCM, people moved almost immediately to the workflow, the available data, and what a pilot would require. We had finally found an idea that did not need us to persuade the hospital to care.

Operational AI fits the system because the problem already has an owner, a workflow, and a budget. The startup can create value without first persuading healthcare to organize itself differently. But this comes with a tradeoff. Much of the hard work is also operational: understanding workflows, integrating with existing systems, selling to enterprises, and implementing the product inside them. AI may power the solution without being the main reason the company wins.

Entering any of them

Suppose a founder chooses one of these directions. How do they get inside the market? For a venture-scale company in Saudi healthcare, the large private providers still look like the most useful place to begin. Their scale can support a meaningful product, and one successful deployment can become evidence for the rest of the market. The problem is that there are only a handful of them, and they are precisely the institutions least likely to trust an unknown company.

An operational product also needs more than a buyer. It needs a design partner willing to expose its workflows, data, systems, and people. Without that access, a startup can build a convincing demonstration and little more. Once again, access is not something that comes after the product. It is one of the materials from which the product is built.

There seemed to be two ways through this wall. The startup could begin with an insider: a healthcare operator, respected executive, or institutional cofounder who already carried trust and understood how the system moved. Or the founders could become insiders themselves by learning the operations, building relationships, securing early projects, and earning credibility one deployment at a time.

The first path requires finding a very particular person. We discovered that this could become a startup of its own. The second is more available, but it is measured in years rather than product sprints.

That is why founder-market fit in healthcare is not only about understanding the problem. Early success often depends less on how quickly a team can build an AI prototype than on whether it can enter an institution, earn a champion, and survive the path from interest to deployment. Technical strength matters. It simply matters later than technologists expect.

The company hidden inside the option

At first, these looked like three choices about what to build. They were really choices about what kind of company to become. Frontier medical AI begins as a research institution. A future-healthcare platform begins as a disruptor with enough leverage to change behavior. Operational software begins closer to an enterprise solutions company and tries to become a repeatable product over time.

That last path can reverse the usual startup sequence. Instead of building technology, turning it into a product, and then finding customers, the company may begin with a healthcare problem, perform implementations, become a trusted vendor, notice what repeats, turn that into a product, and only then scale a platform. Relationships are not merely how the product is sold. They help reveal what the product should be.

By then, the most believable local path was becoming clear: enter through an operational problem, build alongside a large provider, and earn the right to productize over time. It could produce a valuable company.

Around that time, someone I respect told me something that stayed with me: in the usual scenario, you will spend the next decade of your life building the company. Finding a path that could work therefore answered only one question. Before deciding whether we wanted it, we had to understand what kind of company that path would actually create.

2.3. What Would the Winning Company Actually Win On?

Suppose we take the most viable option from the previous section: operational AI for large healthcare providers, beginning with a narrow problem such as rejected insurance claims. Now suppose it works. What kind of technology company would the market shape around it?

Calling it an AI software company is accurate, but incomplete. The AI may power the product, while everything that determines whether it creates value happens around it: reaching the right workflow, connecting to the right systems, proving an economic result, and repeating that result elsewhere. Those requirements do more than constrain the product. Repeated across customers, they shape the company built around it.

What would the company win on?

Imagine two startups with similarly capable AI. One has the slightly better model. The other is already connected to the hospital’s systems, understands how the work actually happens, has evidence from a successful deployment, and is trusted by the people who will carry the risk if something goes wrong. Which one is the hospital more likely to choose?

In a demo, the AI is the most visible part. Inside a hospital, it is only one link in a longer chain. Identifying why an insurance claim was rejected is useful, but value appears only when the product can access the claim, fit the team’s process, return the answer where someone can act on it, and prove that more revenue was recovered. The model can work perfectly while that chain fails around it.

The strange thing is that every deployment makes this less of a technical contest. It leaves behind an integration, data, knowledge of the workflow, evidence that the product works, and a reference that makes the next hospital more willing to listen. A competitor might reproduce the visible feature and still be far behind everything built around it.

The result is a different kind of technology advantage. Rather than resting on one breakthrough, it compounds through technology, access, deployment, data, and trust. Each customer makes that whole system harder to reproduce. It can become a powerful moat, but it is different from the deep-tech trajectory we first imagined, where the main advantage would sit inside the technology itself.

How would the company grow?

The first customer does not settle whether the company has a product. In many ways, the second customer does. If the second deployment reuses the first integration, turns workflow differences into configuration, and moves implementation knowledge into the software, the product becomes more scalable. If the team has to redesign the solution around another provider, the company has delivered a second project.

Healthcare makes this test unusually difficult because two providers can have the same problem and still solve it through different systems and processes. The startup may have to begin by making the solution work for one customer, then discover which parts actually repeat. In the clean startup diagram, implementation comes after the product. Healthcare has other plans.

The path may look more like this: solve a healthcare problem, learn to implement it, become a trusted vendor, notice what repeats, turn that into a product, and only then expand into a platform. Implementation is not necessarily a distraction from the company. It may be how the company learns, earns access, and builds things a competitor cannot easily copy.

But custom work has gravity. Every new provider forces the team to decide what belongs in the product, what can remain configuration, and what it must refuse. Otherwise, growth quietly becomes another name for hiring more people to deliver more projects.

A narrow operational product can still become a strong venture, especially when it serves large customers and creates measurable value. But category-defining scale cannot rest on solving one bounded workflow for a handful of providers. The company must expand across workflows, providers, and eventually markets without turning each expansion into another custom project. Only when one deployment makes the next meaningfully easier does the company begin to gain platform leverage.

What would it require from the founders?

Now imagine the founders inside that company. Before a deployment, they need to reach the buyer, earn an internal champion, and align the departments that can stop the project. During it, they need to understand the operation, coordinate the integration, manage the risk, and prove the result. After it, they need to keep an important customer happy without turning every request into permanent custom work.

Technical speed does not make any of this disappear. We could build a functioning prototype in weeks, while gaining access to the environment needed to validate it took months. The model may be ready. The hospital, understandably, may not be.

This was not only a consequence of our inexperience. If trust and deployment create much of the company’s advantage, then progress will often move at the speed of the institution rather than the speed of engineering. Success also brings more of the same work: more relationships, integrations, internal champions, and implementations that still need to become software.

The founders may eventually build a highly leveraged product, but first they must build everything that allows the product to exist inside healthcare. In the process, they become enterprise healthcare operators as much as technologists. For founders who enjoy turning institutional complexity into repeatable software, that work is not a tax on the company. It is the company.

The company we would spend ten years building

Put all of this together and a particular company begins to appear. It enters through a narrow operational problem with clear economics. It combines technology with access, data, deployment, and trust, then tries to turn what repeats across hospitals into software. If it does this well enough, it may eventually become a broad platform.

This can be a valuable, defensible, and very large technology company. Its implementation work may even become its moat. But its recurring difficulty will remain entering institutions, earning trust, managing deployments, and slowly creating leverage from them. Success does not remove that work; it multiplies it.

That was difficult to reconcile with the company we wanted to build. We wanted technical depth to sit at the center of the advantage, software leverage to appear early enough to shape the company, and the hardest recurring problems to come mainly from invention, systems thinking, and product insight. We were willing to learn sales and healthcare operations—a founder who refuses to sell will have a fairly short career. Learning those skills was not the concern. The concern was building a company whose frontier would continue to be set by access, procurement, and implementation rather than by what we could invent.

Yet every pragmatic question made RCM look stronger. Who is the buyer? Where is the measurable return? Can we secure a design partner? How difficult is the integration? Can this repeat? These were the right questions, and every answer made us feel like more responsible founders. The strange part was that we had become better at finding a company that fit the healthcare market while paying less attention to whether it fit us.

The contrast became clearest in what we naturally discussed. With the healthcare company, our imagination stopped at how to move it through the institution: who would buy, who could block it, how we would earn trust, and how we would integrate. Outside healthcare, it ran ahead to what a product might become, which technical questions would open next, and what we might spend years learning to build. That did not validate another idea. It clarified the kind of company that pulled us forward.

So we removed failure from the decision. Assume we secure the design partner, build a useful product, raise capital, and create a valuable, defensible, large company. What would we spend our days doing inside that successful company? And is that what we want to spend the next decade building?

By then, the answer had quietly become clear. RCM was a strong answer to the healthcare market. It was the wrong answer for us. So, we walked away.

3. Why We Still Believe in Healthcare AI—and Why Saudi Could Lead

3.1. Why AI Will Rebuild Healthcare, Not Just Improve It?

There is something strange about healthcare. Health is continuous—we live with our bodies every hour of every day—but healthcare mostly appears in brief encounters. We wait for an appointment, compress months of life into a short conversation, make a decision, and then largely disappear from the system until the next visit. Why did we build it this way?

Why is healthcare organized around the provider?

The simplest answer is that medical intelligence is scarce. Medical knowledge is already widely available. The difficult part is applying it to one patient. A physician has to connect the symptoms, history, tests, and images, then decide what they mean together. That ability is scarce.

It also takes years to develop. A physician can reason about only so many patients in a day, so the system has to divide their attention. Patients travel to where the intelligence is. Appointments divide access to it. Referrals move harder questions to more specialized experts.

The rest of healthcare grows around these moments. A physician orders a test so someone can interpret it later. A record preserves what one expert learned so the next one can continue. Administrative teams coordinate the approvals, referrals, schedules, and payments around each decision. Even the hospital is partly a way to keep intelligence, equipment, and operations close enough to work together.

Seen this way, organizing healthcare around providers is not merely an old habit. It is what you would build if the intelligence everyone needs existed only inside a limited number of people. Once I saw that, a different question became more interesting: What happens if that intelligence begins to scale?

What happens when medical intelligence scales?

My bet is that AI is already medically intelligent in a meaningful sense, and that it will keep improving until it performs a large share of medicine’s cognitive work. This no longer feels like a claim about the distant future. Doctors we met were already using tools such as ChatGPT and OpenEvidence to search the literature, think through difficult cases, and check their own reasoning. They were not handing over responsibility to AI. They were using it because it was already useful.

I could be wrong about how far this goes. AI may improve more slowly than I expect, remain unreliable in difficult cases, or never become an autonomous physician. Fortunately, the argument does not require the strongest version of the forecast.

Medical judgment is not one indivisible act. Before reaching a difficult decision, a physician has to reconstruct the case, notice patterns, test possible explanations, and find what is missing. AI does not have to master the final decision to help with the work leading to it. If it handles enough of that work well, physicians can spend more time on the uncertain cases where their judgment matters most.

In other words, AI does not need to replace the physician before it changes the system. It only needs to make a meaningful part of medical intelligence available beyond the physician’s limited time. Even without another dramatic breakthrough, that process has already begun.

But the system had another problem. A doctor cannot stay with every patient. So healthcare learned to move patients and information from one expert to another. Much of today’s coordination exists to connect those separate encounters. If intelligence is no longer limited to them, how much of that system still makes sense?

How does coordination change when intelligence is no longer scarce?

Consider a familiar sequence. A patient meets a physician, who orders a test. Someone schedules it, another team performs it, the result enters a record, and a clinician eventually interprets it. The patient may then need another appointment or referral. At every handoff, documentation helps the next person rebuild enough context to continue.

This journey is fragmented, but it is not irrational. The experts cannot remain involved continuously. The system brings them in at separate points and carries information between them. Much of healthcare coordination exists to reconnect these brief episodes of intelligence.

Now imagine that an intelligent system remains with the patient between those episodes. It already knows the history, understands why the test was ordered, interprets the result in context, notices what changed, and knows when a physician, laboratory, pharmacy, or hospital needs to enter. The system no longer has to repeatedly move an incomplete case toward someone who can reconstruct it.

Providers do not disappear. Their place in the system changes. Instead of organizing the patient around appointments and handoffs, the system can organize providers and services around a continuously understood patient. Records become less about rebuilding the past. Referrals can carry an interpreted case instead of a pile of documents. Follow-up can begin when the patient needs it, not only when someone remembers.

AI then pushes the change further because it can also perform much of the coordination that remains. It can prepare the note, arrange the next step, and notice when that step never happened. Work that currently passes through several people and systems can become one continuous process.

So AI changes coordination twice. Some of the old coordination is no longer needed. AI can do much of what remains. It first changes what healthcare needs to coordinate, then changes how the coordination happens.

That leads to the real question behind this subsection: What kind of healthcare system becomes possible once both intelligence and coordination change?

What system follows from that?

Healthcare leaders have talked about preventative, personalized, and patient-centric care for years. The vision was never the difficult part. The difficult part was having someone follow every patient closely enough to make it real.

Under the current model, continuous care requires more clinical and administrative attention for every patient, including the many days when nothing needs to happen. Personalization adds even more information for experts to interpret. Prevention asks the system to notice risk between visits, when it is mostly absent. The vision was good. It was simply too expensive and difficult to run at scale.

AI changes that. An intelligent layer can stay with the patient, absorb new information as it appears, and remain quiet when nothing needs attention. When something changes, it can explain why it matters, collect what is missing, and bring in the right person.

Care becomes continuous because interpretation no longer begins only at the appointment. It becomes preventative because the system can notice risk earlier. It becomes personalized because understanding one patient’s full context no longer consumes the same amount of expert time. And it becomes patient-centric because intelligence stays with the patient and brings providers in when they are needed.

I initially thought these were separate directions within healthcare AI. I now think they are different descriptions of the same system. Once medical intelligence scales and coordination becomes software, healthcare can move from isolated encounters toward a continuous relationship between patients, AI, and providers.

In that model, AI increasingly becomes the patient’s continuous digital interface. Providers remain essential wherever care requires a person, a physical facility, or difficult judgment. But they are no longer the only place where the healthcare relationship exists. The system can understand and coordinate care before, during, and after the encounter.

A better model of care is not enough. It also has to be affordable. Care built around AI could extend clinical capacity, remove manual coordination, and notice problems earlier. Things that were previously too expensive to do for every patient may become ordinary. If that produces better care at a lower cost, the old model becomes difficult to defend.

Who builds the new system?

Existing providers will adopt parts of this technology first. Most will begin with tools that fit the current system: a clinical copilot, automated documentation, or a better digital service for patients. Some will eventually reorganize care around intelligence that is always available.

New companies can begin there from day one. Instead of preserving workflows designed for scarce intelligence, they can ask what care would look like if understanding the patient were continuous. Services that made no economic sense when every decision and handoff required a person may suddenly become viable.

The transition does not depend on every hospital agreeing with this vision. New AI-native players will test the economics. If they deliver better outcomes, lower costs, or a much better experience, existing organizations will have to adapt. Otherwise, patients and activity will gradually move elsewhere.

The old model changes when its assumptions stop being competitive. Scarce medical intelligence created provider-centric care and the coordination system around it. Scalable intelligence makes another system possible. AI-powered coordination makes it practical. That is why I believe AI will rebuild healthcare, not just improve it.

Why is that future still difficult to build today?

This brings us back to the apparent contradiction in our startup journey. If this future follows so naturally, why did we conclude that patient-centric care was a weak starting point?

Because the future system and the present market are different things. Today’s providers still control patient access, clinical data, physical delivery, budgets, and much of the infrastructure a startup needs. A company building the future must find a business model that changes these incentives. Otherwise, it has to persuade the current system to adopt a structure that may eventually weaken it. That is disruption, not ordinary market entry.

My mistake was assuming that because patient-centric care was likely to be the future of healthcare, it was therefore the best startup opportunity today. The destination may be visible while the road remains closed. The long-term thesis can be right while the present market is still unready.

3.2. Why Saudi Is Positioned to Lead Healthcare AI?

If AI rebuilds healthcare, which country will lead that transformation? The obvious answer is one of the countries building the most advanced models. That sounds reasonable until you follow a model out of the laboratory and toward an actual patient.

Where will the healthcare AI race actually be won?

Suppose someone builds an exceptional medical AI system. It reasons across a patient’s history, tests, and images, then recommends a better diagnosis or treatment. The technical breakthrough has happened. Healthcare has barely changed.

The model still has to cross the distance between a benchmark and a patient. That requires clinical data and local validation. Someone must decide how it fits into care, who carries the risk, and why the provider should pay for the change.

Then suppose it succeeds inside one excellent hospital. The country still has one deployment, not a transformed system. If every hospital has to reopen the same questions about data, validation, integration, and incentives, the breakthrough remains trapped where it began.

This is the same gap we encountered as a startup, only at national scale. The model contains the intelligence. The healthcare system decides whether it reaches patients. A country can lead medical AI research and still be slow at turning it into everyday care.

There are really two races. The first is to build the best models. The second is to make those models work across an actual healthcare system. The second race requires a different kind of national capability. What would a country need to win it?

What does a country need to lead?

The first condition is pressure. Healthcare systems do not redesign themselves because a better technology appears. Transformation is expensive, disruptive, and politically difficult. As long as the current model remains good enough, institutions usually improve it at the edges.

The calculation changes when the current system can no longer keep up. Rising costs consume more of the budget while demand grows faster than clinical capacity. Access then becomes harder even as spending increases. At that point, preserving the model begins to look riskier than changing it. Without that pressure, AI remains a collection of useful projects inside the old system.

But pressure can also produce crisis. Many countries know their healthcare systems are becoming unsustainable and still cannot redesign them. The organizations paying for care, delivering it, and regulating it often want different things. Everyone sees part of the problem, but no one can move the whole system.

So pressure is not enough. Someone still has to align the institutions, rewrite rules when they stop making sense, and build infrastructure everyone can use. More importantly, the country has to do this before the benefits are certain. One way to describe that is system-transformation capacity: the ability to move a complex system, not merely announce where it should go.

Few countries have both conditions. Pressure without the capacity to change creates a reactive system. It keeps responding to the latest shortage or budget problem. Capacity without pressure creates an incremental one. It could change, but has no reason to accept the disruption. A country can lead only when it has a reason to move and the ability to move together.

This also changes how we should think about the AI race. Frontier models can travel through partnerships and products. The difficult local work cannot. Each country still has to validate the technology, decide how it will be used, change providers, and spread what works. A country does not need to own every layer of AI. It needs to own the layers where progress gets stuck.

Once the race is defined this way, Saudi Arabia’s position starts to look very different. Does the country actually possess both conditions?

Why does Saudi have an unusual advantage?

When I applied those two questions to Saudi—do we have a reason to change, and can we actually move the system?—our position in the race started to look very different.

Start with pressure. The government finances and delivers much of healthcare, so it cannot pass rising demand and cost to someone else. But this also means that when prevention reduces demand, AI expands clinical capacity, or administration becomes cheaper, much of the benefit returns to the same national system. The actor carrying the long-term problem also captures much of the value from solving it.

Then look at who would have to act. In a fragmented system, the insurer may control payment, hospitals control delivery, regulators control risk, and the technology infrastructure sits somewhere else. Saudi has different institutions too, but they already move under one national transformation. The Health Holding Company can reorganize delivery through twenty clusters, the Council of Health Insurance can change how care is financed, the SFDA can shape the regulatory path, and national technology institutions can provide the digital foundation. No one controls the whole transformation, but the country can bring its pieces into the same room.

The size happens to help too. Twenty clusters are different enough to test whether a model works beyond one excellent hospital, yet they still belong to the same national system. Something that works in one place has a believable route to becoming more than another isolated pilot.

Finally, AI is arriving while that architecture is still being redesigned. The clusters, financing model, digital platforms, and direction toward accountable care are not settled pieces of an old system. We can design AI into what is emerging instead of trying to force it into a model that hardened decades ago.

The reason to move and the ability to move therefore meet in the same place. That is the opening. But an opening is not yet a healthcare AI ecosystem. How much of the practical foundation already exists?

Can Saudi turn the advantage into execution?

First, healthcare does not have to persuade the country that AI matters. Saudi created SDAIA when data and AI became a strategic priority, then created HUMAIN to build across the AI stack. Compute, cloud infrastructure, talent, research, and global partnerships are already being developed for reasons much larger than healthcare. A healthcare founder gets to begin inside that movement rather than create it.

The digital foundation is not theoretical either. Saudi already runs national digital identity and shared government platforms. Within healthcare, NPHIES required providers and insurers to agree on standards and transact through one system, reaching one hundred million insurance transactions by 2023. No founder wakes up dreaming about interoperability standards, including us, but without them every deployment begins again from the plumbing.

This is what makes those investments useful to a founder. They leave things behind. The next healthcare AI company can inherit platforms, standards, institutions, and a growing technical ecosystem instead of rebuilding every layer alone.

Then there is healthcare itself. Before entering it, I quietly expected to find a system that needed technologists to explain what AI could make possible. Instead, we met leaders who understood both its potential and the reasons it usually fails inside an institution. Doctors and operators were ambitious, but painfully precise about workflows, incentives, safety, and implementation. I often entered a meeting expecting to explain the technology and left with a much better explanation of the system.

What they discussed was equally revealing. Prevention, integrated care, population health, patient experience, productivity, and sustainability were already part of the sector’s transformation before the current AI wave. The SFDA was already developing guidance for AI-enabled medical devices. The questions AI forces onto healthcare—how to redesign care, evaluate risk, and coordinate institutions—were not arriving in an empty room.

This is why my optimism is not based only on national ambition. The country can move the system, the wider AI foundation is already being built, and the people inside healthcare are already asking the right questions. We do not need to train every frontier medical model here. We can become unusually good at turning the best models into real healthcare. The remaining question is what would make that easier for the people trying to build it.

3.3. What Moves Could Accelerate the Local Ecosystem?

When I look back at our journey, I do not think about what went wrong. I think about what it would take for the next Saudi startup to get it right.

We already have the ambition and much of the foundation. The remaining work is to translate them into realities that founders can build on.

These are not exhaustive studies. They are simply ideas that came from trying to build, offered as a proud citizen who wants to see our ecosystem reach its potential.

Closing the gap between code and clinic

  • Clinical AI Translation Partnerships: The most advanced medical AI rarely emerges from technology alone. Providers hold the problems, workflows, and clinical data; researchers can push the science forward; and technology companies—whether HUMAIN or an agile startup—can turn the result into a product. Bringing the three together around selected medical problems could create a direct path from clinical need to research, validation, and deployment. The value would be more than better collaboration: it could produce locally validated technology and defensible medical AI companies rather than research that remains in papers or products built on shallow assumptions.

  • National Healthcare Design Partner Network: Our biggest practical obstacle was not building another prototype. It was finding a provider willing to help turn one into a real product. I love what MNGHA and Monsha’at have started with their Healthcare AI Sandbox. A wider network of public clusters and major private providers could openly commit clinical champions, workflow access, and a path to pilots for selected startups. This would shorten the distance between an idea and clinical validation, while helping hospitals shape products around their real needs and giving founders the first credible deployment the rest of the market requires.

  • Clinical Technology Residency Program: Interviews taught us a great deal, including how much an interview can miss. A six-month residency could place AI engineers, researchers, and founders inside clinical and operational teams, where they experience the work rather than hear it described. That proximity would help technologists find higher-value problems, design around real workflows, and earn trust before asking people to change how they work. It could also create a generation of technical talent that understands healthcare deeply enough to build within it.

  • Healthcare Venture-Building Platforms: Healthcare companies need more than capital. They need clinical access, regulatory knowledge, experienced operators, patient investors, and a route to validation. Sanabil’s partnership with Redesign Health is exciting because it assembles these pieces around the company from the beginning. More venture-building platforms could produce startups that are born with stronger domain knowledge, compliance, distribution, and capital—removing years of institutional groundwork from each founding team and increasing the chance that strong ideas survive long enough to scale.

Turning integration and data into shared infrastructure

  • Unified Healthcare AI Sandbox: We often needed several conversations just to learn whether an idea could be tested. I still think the fintech sandbox is one of the smartest things SAMA did: it allowed innovators to experiment while the regulator learned alongside them. A healthcare equivalent could bring safe data access, regulatory guidance, clinical evaluation, and evidence generation into one controlled environment. Founders would reach a credible test faster, regulators would learn from real systems before adoption spreads, and patient safety could remain untouchable throughout.

  • NPHIES Innovation Platform: Every provider-specific integration threatened to turn reusable software into custom work. NPHIES has already done the difficult job of bringing providers and insurers onto common standards. Building clear APIs, documentation, and development environments around it could turn that backbone into a platform for innovation. A startup could integrate once, carry more of the work to the next provider, and spend its engineering effort improving the product rather than reconnecting the same pipes. That is the kind of infrastructure that converts local pilots into scalable companies.

Helping the system buy innovation

  • Public Healthcare Innovation Procurement Path: Existing government procurement frameworks play an essential role in protecting public resources, ensuring transparency, and selecting partners capable of delivering important national projects. Early-stage innovation introduces a different situation: the technology is still being tested, the company may be small, and the first commitment should remain limited. A complementary pathway designed for this stage could begin with a controlled pilot, use clear technical and safety evaluation, and allow the commercial commitment to grow as evidence develops. If the pilot succeeds, the same pathway could provide a transparent transition toward a larger contract. This would preserve the safeguards of public procurement while giving promising local technology a practical route from experimentation to adoption.

  • Private Healthcare Innovation Incentives: Private providers naturally adopt technology when the expected value justifies the cost and operational risk. Emerging healthcare technology can struggle at this stage because its value is still being proven, may appear over a longer period, or may benefit parts of the system beyond the provider making the first investment. The wider opportunity is to design incentives that bring more of that value back to early adopters. This could take several forms: government-supported pilots, co-investment in testing, reimbursement for new models of care, outcome-based rewards, or recognition for providers that serve as serious design partners. The mechanism can vary; the goal is to make testing useful technology economically rational until enough evidence exists for ordinary market demand to take over.

  • National Calls for Healthcare Startups: Y Combinator occasionally publishes “Requests for Startups” around problems it believes are important. I would love to see major Saudi providers and public institutions do something similar: publish an important healthcare problem, explain the available context and desired outcome, and actively invite founders to build companies or solutions around it. The institution could then offer expert access or a path to a design partnership for the strongest teams. Unlike an RFP, the call would define the goal without assuming the answer. It would give founders a strong demand signal while directing technical talent toward national healthcare priorities.

  • Healthcare Founder Access Program: Much of our best learning came from fortunate introductions. Monsha’at already does an excellent job using office hours to connect founders with legal and financial experts. A healthcare version could give founders regular access to MOH leaders, SFDA officials, clinicians, and operators. One honest conversation can prevent months of building around a false assumption. Repeating that access across the ecosystem would help startups converge on real problems faster and make regulatory and clinical knowledge available through a clear channel rather than personal luck.

4. The Story of Our Exploration

So, how did we end up in healthcare?

It started casually. My college friend Mohammed and I both come from deep AI backgrounds. We had no link to healthcare, no medical network, and no real understanding of how hospitals operated. We just knew we wanted to build a technology startup together, and we started looking for interesting problems.

At the time, I was doing my master’s research under Professor Robert, whose lab focused on neurosymbolic AI and bioinformatics. I was strictly on the AI side and had almost zero interest in the biology side. But in casual side chats with the professor and my colleagues, I kept hearing about the massive potential for AI in medicine. One conversation with Professor Robert stands out. We were discussing neurosymbolic AI, and he mentioned a preliminary idea for applying it to medical diagnosis.

That was the spark. I started looking into the global landscape of AI in healthcare, and the momentum was undeniable. There was incredible activity in research and growing commercial traction in the US. I went back to Mohammed and told him this was a space worth exploring.

Looking back, our initial approach was driven by our engineering ambitions. I was deeply immersed in my master’s thesis and wanted to build frontier, deep technology—not a simple software wrapper. Because we were looking at the Saudi market from the outside, we assumed that our AI skills would allow us to walk in and build something highly impactful.

We mapped the market into two broad categories: deep medical models, such as diagnosis and personalized medicine, and operational automation. Then we created our “master plan.” The primary stream would be the deep-tech “System,” likely something in diagnosis. Because that would require immense time and resources, we would fund it through an “Automation” stream: build something fast, monetize the low-hanging fruit, and use the cash to develop the real deep technology in the background.

It was a perfectly logical plan for a standard software market. But we did not know anything about healthcare. Before committing to either stream, we decided to immerse ourselves in the market and learn from the experts.

The echo chamber of users

Because we came from a completely different circle, our personal networks did not include hospital executives or high-profile healthcare leaders. We barely knew a few doctors. So, we did what outsiders do: we reached out to everyone we knew and asked for introductions to anyone who worked in a hospital.

We gathered a decent list of contacts and spent weeks interviewing them about their daily problems. What we did not realize was that almost all of them were doctors and clinical practitioners. This created a blind spot that would shape our thinking for months: we were learning almost entirely about the user’s problems while remaining blind to the buyer’s.

From those interviews, we started testing our two streams. On the deep-tech side, we explored ideas ranging from rare genetic diseases to general diagnostic assistants. The science was fascinating, but the business was difficult to make work. Narrow problems left us with a tiny local market; broader ones required years of research, specialized talent, clinical data, and competition with the largest technology companies.

So, we shifted toward automation. Internal hospital operations appeared too complex and varied for outsiders to enter quickly. The patient journey was easier to observe, and one complaint appeared in almost every conversation: doctors hated the time they spent on documentation and administrative work. We assumed hospital administrators would want to remove it too.

We narrowed the journey further. Emergency and operating rooms were too critical and time-sensitive for a first product. In-patient care varied too much from case to case. Out-patient clinics were systematic, repetitive, and structured. We had found our target.

Could ambient AI be our way in?

We began looking at ambient AI: systems that listen to the conversation between a doctor and a patient and write the clinical note automatically. The problem was obvious, but so were the flaws in our plan. Companies around the world were already solving it, the technical barrier was relatively low, and it was difficult to build a defensible advantage. More importantly, documentation was a major priority for doctors, but perhaps not for the hospital executives who held the budgets.

Still, the problem remained largely unsolved in the Saudi market. We thought excellent user experience and seamless local integration could become our wedge. A documentation tool could get us inside the hospital, help us build trust, and eventually give us the data and access needed for the deeper systems we actually wanted to build.

At that point, we knew our feedback loop was broken. We needed to stop talking only to doctors and start talking to buyers. But an abstract conversation or a slide deck was unlikely to get us very far. To make the discussion specific—and to be taken seriously—we needed something real.

Four weeks to make it real

The Global Health Exhibition in Riyadh was approaching, so we used it as a forcing function. We gave ourselves four weeks to build a prototype for out-patient documentation automation. It would ground our conversations with buyers, demonstrate that we could build, and test whether the market cared. If they loved it, we had our wedge. If they did not, we would lose only four weeks and keep much of the technical infrastructure.

Because note-taking alone felt basic, our grand design covered the full patient journey. Before the visit, an AI agent would collect the patient’s history and prepare the doctor. During the visit, ambient AI would create the notes, orders, and medical coding. Around both, rules could reduce unnecessary tests for public hospitals and catch missed billing or claim errors for private ones. The idea was to solve the user’s problem while creating measurable value for the buyer.

Of course, the reality of a four-week sprint set in. We could not build all of that. We had to choose the part that best showed a differentiated AI experience rather than another ambient listening tool.

The prototype we took to the exhibition was much narrower: a web and phone app where patients could complete their pre-visit intake through text or audio. The AI processed the conversation and sent a clean summary to the doctor’s dashboard alongside the patient’s medical history.

It was a fraction of the grand vision, but it was real, it was built, and we were ready to put it in front of the market.

What did the exhibition teach us?

We did not close any deals at the exhibition, but the density of learning was incredible. Most encounters were short pitches followed by exchanging business cards for future demos. Almost all of those cards became dead leads. The few longer conversations, however, taught us two important lessons about the market.

The first was how much trust mattered. Healthcare buyers were naturally skeptical, and we were two young, pre-seed, first-time founders with no healthcare background, no clients, and an untested prototype. Promising technical skills were not enough. Signing the first hospital would require institutional trust that we simply did not have.

The second was a strange paradox around ambient AI. Everyone knew about the documentation problem, and everyone seemed to be building a note-taking tool. Yet despite the competition and the constant complaints from doctors, local adoption remained incredibly low. I could not explain it at the time, but I sensed that something beyond the technology was preventing adoption.

So, why not build the future instead?

Rather than continue fighting in the red ocean of ambient AI, we stepped back and divided the market into two broad directions: workflow automation and patient-centric care.

Workflow automation felt tied to legacy systems and crowded categories. Winning there appeared to depend less on building frontier AI and more on integration, buyer relationships, pricing, and operational execution. Patient-centric care felt like the opposite. It meant moving care beyond the clinic through continuous and preventative engagement. One looked like fighting over the past; the other looked like building the future.

Naturally, we chose the future. It matched our ambition to build complex technical systems and aligned with the direction of Saudi Arabia’s health transformation.

To make sure we were not choosing purely based on excitement, we built a four-point framework. We asked whether an idea created clear value for the provider, whether patients would use it, how difficult it would be to implement, and whether it could become defensible. Based on this very rigorous framework, we found our new wedge: Front Desk AI.

The product would be a conversational AI agent coordinating the experience before and after care: access, intake, navigation, and follow-up. The wedge gave us two things we believed were strategically important—direct access to the patient and integration with the provider. Together, those could become the foundation for a much larger patient-centric health platform. On paper, it connected beautifully.

The wall of validation

With the patient-centric thesis and a defined wedge, we set out to validate it. The exhibition leads went entirely cold. No one showed enough interest to move toward a pilot, and we had no clear idea how to get inside a hospital ourselves.

Around the same time, we explored the idea with a healthcare venture studio that liked the patient-centric vision. The next step was clear: validate it with providers.

During that messy period, I asked a VC partner for advice. He listened to my thesis on ambient AI and Front Desk AI, then told me something that stayed with me: “Your understanding of these ideas is entirely first-order. As you spend more time immersing yourself in this ecosystem, you are going to pivot again.”

I did not fully understand what he meant. But he was entirely right.

What did the market actually look like?

We returned to our personal circle, asked for introductions, and thankfully, people helped us. We finally managed to sit down with senior healthcare executives. Those conversations, combined with deeper research, revealed a market very different from the one we had imagined from the outside.

The public sector represented most of the market and had a genuine appetite for technology. But public procurement was designed around the risk and scale of public contracts, not the speed and uncertainty of a pre-seed startup. It could become an excellent place to validate proven technology, but we could not build our first sales strategy around it.

The private market seemed easier until we looked at its shape. Small and medium providers were numerous and more accessible, but their budgets, problems, workflows, and systems varied widely. Winning ten small customers could require ten different sales efforts and ten different integrations. We might build a respectable business there, but not necessarily the scalable technology company we had in mind.

That left the large private providers: enough budget and scale to matter, but only a small number of possible first customers. Their infrastructure narrowed the list further. Some used closed global systems, others used local systems, and a few owned their own. An integration with one provider could become a reusable foundation—or months of work that helped us nowhere else. By the time we applied all these filters, a market that had looked enormous had shrunk to a handful of plausible first clients.

But what would they actually buy?

Finding the right provider still did not make our idea an opportunity. We also had to understand what happened inside a single hospital. This was where the mystery around ambient AI finally began to make sense.

The doctor clearly benefited from spending less time on documentation. But the doctor did not control the systems, approve the integration, or hold the budget. The medical office cared about physician experience and quality. IT had to protect sensitive data and critical workflows. Finance needed an economic reason to pay. The same product could be helpful to one stakeholder, risky to another, and financially irrelevant to a third.

Even the obvious return depended on a hidden assumption. Ambient AI promised to save time so doctors could see more patients. That created revenue only when their schedules were already full. If an empty slot remained empty, saving three minutes on documentation did not produce another appointment.

The incentives changed again in the public sector. High patient demand made shorter encounters more valuable to the administration, but a doctor on fixed compensation might reasonably see the same time saving as additional workload. The technology had not changed. The surrounding system had, and with it the opportunity.

Front Desk AI had a similar problem. Automating calls sounded useful, but call handling was not always a major cost, while replacing it touched several departments and workflows. The value was modest; the organizational change was not.

The uncomfortable correction

Earlier in the journey, I had quietly assumed healthcare administrators were old-fashioned, bureaucratic, and resistant to innovation. By this point, that explanation no longer survived what we had seen.

Healthcare leaders were being asked to trust young companies with sensitive data, important workflows, and promises made by people who often understood very little about how a hospital actually worked. Today, a convincing AI prototype can be built in a few hours. That makes demos easier to produce, but it does not make them easier to trust. If anything, it creates more noise for the people responsible when something goes wrong.

The slowness was not separate from the system; it was a rational response to its complexity and risk. The problem was not that healthcare had failed to understand our ideas. We had failed to understand healthcare.

Our patient-centric vision still looked beautiful from the future. It simply did not fit how the system operated and bought today. If we wanted to continue, ambition was no longer enough. We needed a plan that began with the market as it was.

So, what would actually work?

Once we mapped the market this way, the question became much stricter. What problem had a clear buyer, measurable value, aligned stakeholders, and a realistic place for AI?

The answer was Revenue Cycle Management (RCM), specifically managing rejected insurance claims. A coding or documentation error could turn revenue the hospital had already earned into a visible loss. The RCM team owned the problem, finance could measure the improvement, and AI was well suited to finding the errors and patterns behind it.

We formed a thesis and started pitching it. For the first time in the journey, people were genuinely listening. We had two promising leads: a large provider that could become an exceptional design partner and a mid-sized one that offered a more realistic starting point. The next steps were no longer mysterious. Validate the problem, build the product with a provider, establish credibility, and expand from there.

We had solved the puzzle. We had found the pragmatic answer.

Then why did something still feel wrong?

Enterprise healthcare moves slowly, even when there is interest. Some days, the only thing we could do was wait for someone to answer an email. Mohammed started building side projects just to have something to code. I started questioning everything.

Mostly as a contingency plan, we began discussing ideas outside healthcare. The change in our conversations was immediate. We returned naturally to architectures, experiments, strange product ideas, and technical roadmaps that seemed to extend on their own. I remember telling Mohammed that I had not seen him this excited in a while. It took me slightly longer to notice the same thing about myself.

The contrast was difficult to ignore. The healthcare opportunity that survived our filters pulled our conversations toward buyers, design partners, procurement, credibility, and integrations. Other ideas pulled them toward invention: what could exist, how it might work, and what we could spend years learning to build.

None of this made RCM a bad opportunity. Excitement is not market validation; founders are perfectly capable of becoming excited about terrible ideas! But it revealed something our frameworks could not measure. We had spent months asking whether an opportunity could become a business. We had not asked carefully enough what kind of business it would become—or what kind of founders it would require us to become.

The ten-year question

At some point, I stopped imagining the first product and started imagining the next ten years of my life. I saw the relationships, procurement, stakeholder coordination, and integrations that succeeding in healthcare would require. We could learn to do those things. The harder question was whether we wanted them to remain the center of the company we were building.

What pulled us toward startups in the first place was different. We wanted to build something technically ambitious, move quickly, experiment, and spend years surrounded by exceptional people solving problems that kept unfolding. We were comfortable with hard work. We had simply discovered that not all hard work leads to the same life.

The question was no longer, “Can we build a successful healthcare startup?” It had become, “Assuming this path works, is this the company we want to spend the next decade building?”

By then, the answer had quietly become clear. We had paid the tuition, understood the market, and found a viable path forward. Then we looked down that path and decided to walk away.
